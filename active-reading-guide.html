The Active Reading Guide

Reading a chapter and remembering a chapter are different skills. This guide covers what to do before, during and after you read so that the ideas are still there next month.

Why passive reading fades

When your eyes move across a page, it feels like learning. The words make sense, the argument follows, and you nod along. A week later you can recall the cover and perhaps one example. That is not a memory problem. It is what happens when nothing was asked of your memory in the first place. Active reading simply means giving your mind a job to do while you read, and another one afterwards.

Before: preview for five minutes

Do not start at the first word. Spend five minutes finding out what the chapter is going to do.

  1. Read the title, the opening paragraph and the closing paragraph.
  2. Skim the headings and anything in bold.
  3. Look at diagrams, tables and their captions.
  4. Write down two or three questions you expect the chapter to answer.

Those questions matter more than they seem. Reading to answer a question is a different activity from reading because the page is in front of you.

During: mark less, think more

A highlighter is the least useful tool in a pencil case. Colouring a sentence takes no thought, and a page that is mostly yellow tells you nothing later. Use a pencil and three marks only:

  • A line in the margin beside a key claim.
  • A question mark where you disagree or do not follow.
  • A star for the one idea on the page you would want to keep.

Then write a few words beside the mark in your own language. If you cannot put the point in your own words, you have found the place you need to reread, and that is useful to know.

After: close the book and recall

This is the step that does most of the work, and it takes two minutes. Shut the book. On a blank page, or out loud, answer three things from memory:

  1. What was the main point?
  2. What evidence or example supported it?
  3. What do I still not understand?

Only then open the book to check. The effort of pulling the idea back out is what fixes it in place. It will feel harder than rereading. That difficulty is the method working.

Later: a five-minute review routine

One recall session is good. Three spaced ones are far better, and each takes less time than the last.

WhenWhat to doTime
Same dayRecall the main points without looking, then check2 minutes
Two days laterRead only your margin notes; explain each one aloud5 minutes
One week laterWrite a five-sentence summary from memory5 minutes
One month laterReread your summary; add anything you have since learned3 minutes

Keep a one-line reading log

For every chapter or sitting, write one line: the date, the book, and a single sentence about what you took from it. A year of these fits in a thin notebook, and reading back through it is the fastest way to recover a book you thought you had forgotten.

For fiction, the line might be a moment or a sentence you liked. For non-fiction, the claim you would repeat to a friend. Keep it to one line; a log that takes ten minutes will not survive a busy week.

Adjusting for different material

A novel does not need the full routine. Recall where you were before you start and write your one line when you stop. A textbook deserves all of it, chapter by chapter. Articles and reports sit in between: preview, read once with a pencil, and write three sentences at the end. Match the effort to how much you need to keep.
