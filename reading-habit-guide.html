The Reading Habit Guide

Most people who say they have no time to read have about forty spare minutes a day. This guide is about finding them and making reading the easiest thing to do with them.

In this guide

  1. Start smaller than feels useful
  2. Attach reading to something you already do
  3. Choose books you want, not books you should
  4. Make the phone harder to reach
  5. Track pages, not books
  6. When you fall off

Start smaller than feels useful

The usual plan is an hour every evening, and it lasts until the first busy week. A better plan is one you could keep on your worst day. Ten pages, or fifteen minutes, whichever comes first. On good days you will carry on well past it. On bad days you will still have read, and the chain stays unbroken.

Ten pages sounds like nothing. Over a year it is 3,650 pages, or about a dozen ordinary books. The Reading Goal Planner will do that sum for any target you like.

Attach reading to something you already do

A habit needs a cue, and the most reliable cue is another habit. Pick one fixed moment in the day and put the book there, physically.

  • With the first hot drink. The book lives beside the kettle.
  • On the commute. The book lives in the bag, not on the shelf.
  • After lunch. Fifteen minutes before going back to the desk.
  • In bed. The phone charges in another room; the book is on the pillow.

One slot is enough. People who try to read at every spare moment usually end up reading at none of them.

Choose books you want, not books you should

Nothing ends a reading habit faster than a worthy book you are not enjoying. In the first three months, read only what pulls you forward: thrillers, memoirs, a subject you are curious about, a favourite from years ago. The aim at this stage is to make reading the thing you look forward to. Harder books can come later, and they will be easier once the habit carries you to the chair.

Apply the fifty-page rule. If a book has not earned your attention by page fifty, set it aside without guilt and open the next one.

Make the phone harder to reach

Reading rarely loses to work or family. It loses to the phone, because the phone is in your hand and the book is across the room. Reverse that. Leave the phone to charge in the hallway, log out of the one app that eats your evenings, and keep the book where the phone used to sit. You do not need willpower if the easier option is the one you want.

Track pages, not books

Counting finished books rewards short, easy reads and makes a long novel feel like failure. Count pages or minutes instead. A simple grid on paper, one box a day, with the number of pages written in, is enough. Seeing a row of filled boxes is a small reward in itself, and a gap is a prompt, not a verdict.

When you fall off

Everyone does. Illness, a deadline, a book that turned out to be dull. The rule that rescues most habits is simple: never miss twice. One skipped day is rest. Two is the start of a new habit of not reading. If you have been away for weeks, do not go back to the book you abandoned. Start something short and enjoyable, read ten pages tonight, and begin the chain again.
